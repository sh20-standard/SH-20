# Audit, part `sealing`: keys, addresses, ciphertexts, scanning (2026-11-24)

Run from [`docs/AUDIT-PROMPT.md`](../AUDIT-PROMPT.md), part `sealing`, by the circuit session
(Fable 5.2). **That session wrote `docs/SPEC-NOTES.md`, `circuit/js` and `circuit/src/notes.rs`.**
The prompt asks for an auditor who did not. What was done about it: a second reading by a fresh
agent of the same model, which had the code, the spec and the prompt and none of this session's
notes (§6). Both are sessions of one kind, so the blind spot the prompt names remains, and the
audit by people that mainnet waits for is not replaced.

**Result: one High, two Medium, six Low, two Notes. No finding lets anyone take or forge a
note.** What an output carries is bound bit for bit to its leaf, its ephemeral key and its
recipient; nothing but a note the wallet can spend, for the amount and the ticker shown, passes a
scan; the three implementations compute RFC 7748 on every encoding, on OpenSSL, Chrome and
x25519-dalek; no key and nonce serve twice.
What fails is around the scan:

- **High.** How long a scan takes tells the wallet's indexer whether the wallet holds an address,
  and how many of the outputs it was served are its own (sealing-1).
- **Medium.** One output with one of five values of `pk_eph` makes the scan throw in Firefox, at
  every sync, in every vault (sealing-2). Notes sent to a vault grow one record until it can no
  longer be saved (sealing-3).

**The three were found by the second reading, not by this session** (§6): the author of the code
had read past them, which is what the prompt warns of. Each is reproduced here by a test, and the
Firefox behaviour was run again in Firefox 160. sealing-1 to sealing-3 should be fixed before the
signet is announced; none needs a change to the circuit, the keys or the wire.

## 2. Properties

Written from `SPEC-NOTES.md` before the code was read again.

1. **Separation.** Every derived value has its own tag and input: `m`, `ask`, `nsk`, `ivk`, a
   note's `s`, `τa`, `τn`, `rho`, the key and nonce of its ciphertext, the secret of a public
   note, the nonce of a signature. No value serves two wallets, two notes or two roles.
2. **Addresses.** A payer refuses what §2 lists: case, checksum, prefix, length, version, padding;
   `A` and `N` that are not canonical points of the subgroup of order `l`, or the identity; an
   `IVK` of low order.
3. **X25519.** It is the function of RFC 7748 on every 32 bytes: bit 255 ignored, a value of `p`
   and above taken modulo `p`, zeros for the points of low order and only for them. The three
   implementations agree on every encoding.
4. **AES-GCM.** No (key, nonce) pair serves two plaintexts, whatever the sender reuses.
5. **What the tag binds.** A ciphertext opens under its own leaf, its own ephemeral key and its
   recipient's key, and under nothing else; what opens is spendable for the amount and the ticker
   shown, or it is ignored (the leaf check).
6. **Scanning.** An ephemeral key of low order is skipped from its bytes; a failure of the
   library is an error, never a skipped note; a note is named by its position.
7. **What a scan costs, and what stops it.** No output makes a scan throw, stall or cost more
   than one key agreement and one decryption.
8. **What an output reveals.** Nothing of the recipient, the amount or the ticker to anyone but
   the sender and the holder of `ivk`; no two outputs to one address are linked; the sender of a
   note cannot tell when it is spent.
9. **Public notes.** Derived from `ivk` and a counter that never repeats; `rho = 0`.
10. **Unused inputs and outputs.** Made as §3 says, from fresh randomness of the platform.
11. **What an indexer learns.** The wallet reads the notes and the nullifiers whole and asks
    about none of its own.
12. **The signature.** Its nonce follows from the key and the message and from nothing else.
13. **Wire v0.4, on paper.** One ephemeral key for the outputs of a spend keeps 3, 4, 5 and 8.

**sealing-1.** Three facts, each harmless alone.
- A scan costs 0.1 to 0.3 ms when the tag of the ciphertext fails, and about 0.2 s when it
  verifies: the leaf check that follows needs the note's `npk`, two scalar multiplications on
  BabyJubJub in JavaScript integers (`webcrypto.js` l. 158–162).
- The `pkEph` and the `ct` of a leaf are what the indexer says they are. The root covers the leaf,
  and the digest's record of a spend holds nullifiers and positions (`SPEC-WIRE.md` §6).
- The vault's next request to its indexer comes after the scan: `/status` ends the sync
  (`sync.js` l. 147), the scan runs (`notes-panel.js` l. 164), then `/listings` is asked
  (l. 178). The time between the two is the scan, to within the network's jitter.

So an indexer that knows an address, and addresses are meant to be published, serves a client
leaves that are in the tree with a `pkEph` and a `ct` it made for that address: the tag verifies
for the owner of the address and for nobody else. The owner computes for seconds and the leaf
check then refuses each one: nothing is recorded, nothing is shown, and it works again at the
next sync. Any other client answers in milliseconds. It costs the indexer no transaction. Without
serving anything false, the same clock counts the outputs of the last blocks that are the
client's own: each one is 0.2 s the first time, and its unused outputs of value 0 are 0.2 s at
every sync that reads them again. With few spends in a block, that names the spend.

`SPEC-NOTES.md` §4 says the wallet "never asks an indexer about one of its own notes", and the
header of `sync.js` that the indexer never learns which notes are the user's. Against a clock
both fail: this is a loss of what a spend hides, its recipient, to the indexer the wallet reads,
and to a lesser degree to whoever watches the timing of its connections.

Reproduce: `sealing-1` in `circuit/js/test/audit-sealing.test.js`. Eight outputs made for an
address take 1.3 s to scan for its owner and about 1 ms for another wallet, on both entry points,
and neither finds anything. Not run: a page against an indexer that lies; the three facts were
each checked, and the request order was read.

Fix, in the vault, with no change to the spec: no request may depend on when a scan ends. Ask
`/listings` and whatever else a sync needs before scanning, scan in a worker, and let the next
sync start by the clock. A faster multiplication narrows the gap and does not close it, since the
indexer chooses how many outputs it serves. Comparing `/notes` across indexers (sealing-7) takes
the false bytes away, and the passive count stays.

*The vault's part fixed on 2026-11-24, in the pull request with `sealing-1` in its title: a sync asks
every server what it needs before the scan (the notes held, the spends refused, the listings, the
DEPLOY reveals, the other indexers' `/notes`) and nothing after it, until the next sync, which the user
or an unlock starts (`vault/src/ui/notes-panel.js` `syncOnce`; `vault/test/scan-timing.test.js`). The
scan stays on the page's thread, between WebCrypto calls, so the minute timer of the DEPLOY reveals
waits at most one output's leaf check when it falls during a scan; a worker would need `circuit/js`
without `node:crypto`. The test `sealing-1` above still holds: it measures the scan, which costs what
it did.*

**sealing-2.** `subtle.js` decides a point of low order from the seven encodings of §4, with bit
255 cleared, and hands every other 32 bytes to the platform as they are (l. 93–103). Five byte
strings are not of low order for RFC 7748, which masks bit 255 first, and are in the list of
twelve that some libraries refuse by their raw bytes: `p` plus each point of order 8, `2p − 1`,
`2p` and `2p + 1` as 256-bit integers. OpenSSL, Chrome 158 and x25519-dalek compute a secret for
them. Firefox 160 refuses to import them: `DataError`. The scan of an output that carries one
then throws.

Anyone can publish such an output: `pk_eph` is opaque to the indexers, and a TRANSFER of two
unused inputs and three outputs of 0 needs no token. From then on, in every vault that runs in
Firefox:
- the leaf is in `failed` at every sync, the next sync reads the chain again from it
  (`notes-panel.js` l. 167, `sync.js` l. 117), and each sync is slower than the last;
- a restored vault never ends its first check, which needs a scan with no failure (l. 171–174),
  so it reserves no public note: no MINT, no TAKE, no cancel, no CLAIM.

Reproduce: the page described in §7, in Firefox: `DataError` for the five, the RFC's bytes for
the 47 other odd encodings and for the five once written in canonical form. And `sealing-2` in
the JavaScript test file, where Firefox's refusal is put in front of Node's WebCrypto.

Fix, in `circuit/js` and in no spec: both `x25519` functions hand the platform the canonical
encoding of `u`, bit 255 cleared and `p` subtracted when it is at or above `p`. It is the same
function of RFC 7748, and the bytes hashed into the note's secret stay those of the wire. Then
Safari, which was not run (§5). And in the vault, a leaf that fails every time should not hold
back the others for ever.

*Fixed on 2026-11-24, in the pull request with `sealing-2` in its title: `canonicalU` in
`circuit/js/subtle.js`, used by both `x25519`. The five values are five more known answers of
`test/subtle-kat.js`: in Firefox 160 they fail with the file as it was (`DataError`) and the 44
pass with the fix; the test `sealing-2` now asserts that the scan answers "not for this wallet"
on a WebCrypto that refuses them. The vault's part of the fix, a leaf that keeps failing, is not
in it.*

**sealing-3.** A note received is appended to `state.notes` (`keys.js` l. 370–376) and never
taken out, spent or not. The whole state is one value of `localStorage` (l. 243–250), about 750
characters a note. At each sync `checkOwned` updates every note held, and each update saves the
whole record (`notes-panel.js` l. 84, `keys.js` l. 449). So the saving of one sync grows with the
square of the number of notes: 40 s for 1,000 notes and 5 minutes for 3,000 on this machine, in
memory. Near 7,000 notes the record passes the 5 MB a browser gives an origin, and every save
throws: a note received is not recorded, and neither is a spend, which is recorded before it is
signed. The vault can no longer empty itself.

Whoever knows an address does this with notes of 1 unit: two for each TRANSFER, with tokens that
the genesis mint gives for nothing. About 3,500 transfers, a little more than one block of fees,
for a vault that stays stopped after a restore too, since a restore finds the same notes. No
funds are lost: the words still spend them, from other software.

Reproduce: `sealing-3` in `vault/test/audit-sealing.test.js`. The 5 MB is the usual quota, not
measured in each browser. Fix: one save for each sync; the notes in IndexedDB, one row each;
spent notes out of what is rewritten. It sits on the border of the part `vault`, and is here
because a sender reaches it through scanning.

*Fixed on 2026-11-24 with vault-3 of `docs/audit/2026-11-24-vault.md`, the same finding: one record a
note in IndexedDB, written when it changes; one save a sync; no path or spent status stored. The test
`sealing-3` now asserts it, past the quota the whole vault in one value met.*

**sealing-4.** `m` is the first half of `HMAC-SHA512("sh20/v0/seed", seed)`, and every key follows
from `m`: `ask`, `nsk`, `ivk`, the secrets of the public notes, and in the vault the keys of the
listing locks (`HMAC-SHA512("sh20/vault/lock", m ‖ index)`). The network enters none of them. It is
only in the prefix of the address, `sh20` or `tsh20`, around the same 97 bytes.

So a set of words used on signet and then on mainnet gives, on both chains:
- the same public openings `npk_0, npk_1, …`, which a MINT, a TAKE and a CLAIM write in the clear;
- the same lock keys, which a LIST writes in the clear;
- the same address, under another prefix.

Whoever reads both chains matches them. A wallet's first MINT on mainnet carries the `npk_0` of its
first MINT on signet, where coins are worth nothing and people take less care. Nothing is stolen
and no amount is shown; two identities that the user may want apart are tied. The signet is where
people are being asked to try the vault first.

Reproduce: the two tests named in the table. Fix, in order of cost. The vault's mainnet build
tells the user to write down new words and not to restore signet ones (no byte changes). Or the
network enters the derivation, for example `m = HMAC-SHA512("sh20/v0/seed", seed ‖ hrp)`, which
changes every key of the wallets that exist: free for mainnet, where none exists, and a choice for
signet. It is a change to `SPEC-NOTES.md` §1, for the spec owner, before the first mainnet wallet.

*Fixed on 2026-11-24, by a decision of the maintainer, in the pull request with `sealing-4` in its
title: `m` is the first 32 bytes of `HMAC-SHA512("sh20/v0/seed", seed ‖ hrp)`, `hrp` the address
prefix of the network, `sh20` or `tsh20`, and a derivation that names neither is refused
(`SPEC-NOTES.md` §1; `master_secret` in `circuit/src/notes.rs`, `masterSecret` in both entry
points of `circuit/js`; the vectors hold each wallet on both networks). The vault and the relayer
pass the prefix of their profile, so the lock keys follow. The two tests of the table now assert
that one seed gives other keys, addresses and public openings on mainnet than on the test
networks. The prefix is in the derivation on the test networks too: words written before the
change give other keys there as well; a vault that exists keeps its `m` in its record and its
keys with it. What the vault says to someone who restores such words is not in that pull
request.*

**sealing-5.** Apart from what sealing-1 makes of it, the 0.2 s is a cost that whoever knows an
address can put on its wallet for the price of an output, about 110 vB. A block filled with them
is half an hour of one wallet's time. An output of value 0 is not remembered (`keys.js` l. 369),
so it is derived again at each sync that reads its leaf, the six last blocks, and at every full
scan and every restore. Fix: remember the positions of outputs that opened with 0 or failed the
leaf check; a fixed-base multiplication with a table.

**sealing-6.** §2 refuses an `IVK` of low order and says nothing of one that is not canonical.
The three decoders accept an `IVK` with bit 255 set, or at `p` and above, and X25519 reads it as
the same key. But `IVK` is also hashed, as written, into the secret of each note, and the owner
scans with its own writing. Every wallet thus has a second address string, valid everywhere,
whose notes it never finds; they open only for a scan told to write `IVK` that second way. It
adds no power to someone who can already replace an address (§6). Fix: §2 and the three decoders
refuse an `IVK` that is not below `p` with bit 255 clear; the same rule for `pk_eph` in §4 would
have kept sealing-2 from the chain. Both are changes to `SPEC-NOTES.md`, for its owner.

*Fixed on 2026-11-24 for `IVK`, by a decision of the maintainer, in the same pull request as
sealing-4: an address has one encoding of `IVK`, below `p` with bit 255 clear (`SPEC-NOTES.md`
§2, check 6). The three decoders refuse the others ("IVK is not in its one encoding"), and `seal`
refuses them in an address built by hand. Two such addresses are in the vectors, fifteen refused
in all. The two tests of the table now assert the refusal, for bit 255 set and for `p` to
`p + 18`, with and without that bit. The rule for `pk_eph` was not decided and §4 is as it was.*

**sealing-7.** The tree commits to the leaf of an output. Its `pkEph` and `ct` come from the
indexer's `/notes` beside it. An indexer that serves other bytes makes the scan answer "not for
this wallet", and the leaf is marked scanned: the note is hidden until a full scan from another
indexer. After a spend, that is the wallet's own change: its inputs show spent and the rest is
not found. It cannot make a wallet accept a note: the leaf check stands between. `docs/AUDIT.md`
§3 says an indexer can hide notes. What it does not say is that the vault holds what it needs not
to depend on it: the envelope of each of its spends, with its three outputs, is in its pending
record. Fix: scan the vault's own pending envelopes from its own bytes; compare `/notes` across
indexers as roots and digests are compared.

**sealing-8.** `receive` answers "known" for a position it has a record for, before it looks at
the leaf (`keys.js` l. 366–367). `checkOwned`, which drops a sealed note whose leaf is no longer
at its position, runs after the scan (`notes-panel.js` l. 164, 176). When a reorg puts another
note of the vault at such a position, the new note is skipped and the old record dropped; the
leaf is scanned again only if it is in the six last blocks of the next sync. Otherwise the note
waits for a full scan. Fix: drop the records that moved before scanning, or replace a record
whose leaf differs.

**sealing-9.** `anchor()` returns the height of the last sync, which is the height the indexer
gave, and accepts it while it is at most 90 blocks behind the tip (`send.js` l. 20, 32–38). The
other indexers are then asked about that same height, and agree. The anchor is public in the
spend. An indexer that keeps one client on a height of its own recognises that client's next
spend, and even an honest one can pair a sync at height `h` with a spend anchored at `h` when
few wallets are active. Fix: warn when the indexer is more than a few blocks behind the chain's
tip, and anchor at a height taken from the tip.

**sealing-10.** What the vault asks its indexers names no note: `/status`, `/notes?from=`,
`/nullifiers?from=`, `/roots`, `/digest`, `/tokens`, `/listings`, and `/token/:ticker` before a
BURN, a LIST or a DEPLOY, which publish their ticker anyway (§4.11). What ties the user to its
spends by name goes to the Esplora server: the unspent outputs of its Bitcoin address, the
broadcast of its commits and reveals, and the status of each reveal it waits for. A user who
wants that hidden points the vault at a node of its own.

**sealing-11.**
- `seal.js` l. 119–124 reads every error of the decipher as "not for this wallet". `webcrypto.js`
  reads only a failed tag that way and throws the rest, which is what §4 asks.
- §1 says a note whose `ask + τa` or `nsk + τn` is 0 is invalid. No scan checks it; the JavaScript
  `spendKeys` throws when asked for such keys (l. 136–138), and the model returns nothing. The
  chance is 2^−250 and a sender cannot aim at it.
- No draw of `crypto.getRandomValues` is checked beyond its length.
- For wire v0.4: a relayer that restores from its seed finds a fee by one note-key derivation,
  0.2 s in this JavaScript, for each spend of the chain before it can try an amount against
  `leaf2`; the section says "one leaf hash for each try" and leaves that out. And with one
  ephemeral key for a spend, showing `e` to prove a payment would open every output of the spend,
  the sender's change included.

## 4. Holds

1. **Separation.** One tag for each derivation (`seal.js` l. 13–24, `eddsa.js` l. 16, the vault's
   `sh20/vault/lock`), all different from one another and from the indexer's. `hs` and `hr` hash
   `tag ‖ 32 bytes ‖ one byte`, the signature's nonce `tag ‖ 64 bytes ‖ one byte`, `ivk` is
   `SHA256(tag ‖ 32 bytes)`: no two of them can hash one string. The secret of a note depends on
   the ephemeral key, the recipient's `IVK` and the Diffie–Hellman value; the key and nonce of its
   ciphertext on that secret and on the leaf; the secret of a public note on `ivk` and its counter.
   `ivk` serves twice, as the X25519 key and as the input of the public notes' HKDF: both are what
   a viewing key is for, and nothing an attacker sees is computed from `ivk` by both. Test: 21
   derived values of a wallet, all different, on both entry points
   (`the derived secrets of a wallet are all different, and so are their tags`). Across networks
   nothing is separated: sealing-1.
2. **Addresses.** `bech32mDecode` (`address.js` l. 150–174) refuses mixed case, a wrong checksum
   and padding; `parseAddress` (l. 188–199) the prefix, the length, the version and any `A` or `N`
   that is not a canonical point of the subgroup, the identity included (`unpackPoint` l. 90–106,
   `isKeyPoint` l. 109–111); `decodeAddress` (l. 205–209, `webcrypto.js` l. 95–99) an `IVK` of low
   order. The vault sends only to what `decodeAddress` returned (`vault/src/send.js`). Tests: the
   thirteen addresses of the vectors, and
   `addresses a payer must refuse, beyond the vectors`: the fourteen encodings of a low-order
   `IVK`, `A` and `N` of order 2, 4 and 8 and with such a part added, the identity, 96 and 98
   bytes, the other prefix, a space, one capital, one padding bit, each with a valid checksum, on
   both entry points; the low-order `IVK` again on the Rust model. One thing §2 does not list is
   accepted, and should not be: an `IVK` written a second way (sealing-6).
3. **X25519.** Both entry points decide a point of low order from its bytes, bit 255 cleared,
   against the seven values of §4 (`subtle.js` l. 66–84), give zeros for it without asking the
   library, and throw if the library gives zeros for any other point (`address.js` l. 35–41,
   `subtle.js` l. 93–103). Tests: against X25519 written again from RFC 7748 §5, the two entry
   points and the Rust model (x25519-dalek) give the same 32 bytes for more than 250 inputs each: the
   fourteen low-order encodings, `p + 2` to `p + 18` with and without bit 255, the ends of the
   range, and 200 random pairs, about half of them on the twist. Zeros come out for the fourteen
   and for nothing else. In Chrome 158 the page `circuit/js/test/subtle.html` passes its 39
   checks, among them "ignores bit 255" and "takes a `u` above the field size modulo `p`"; and
   Chrome's own X25519, asked from a page with the same list, gives the RFC's bytes for the 47
   odd encodings that are not of low order and for 200 random pairs, and an `OperationError` for
   the fourteen of low order, which a wallet never hands it. Firefox 160 gives the same answers on
   that list. On five more values it does not: sealing-2.
4. **AES-GCM.** The key and the nonce are `HKDF(s, info = leaf)`. Two notes share them only if
   they share `s` and the leaf, and one leaf is one amount and one ticker. Test
   `an ephemeral key used again never uses a keystream again`: with one `e`, another amount,
   another ticker, another recipient and an amount that differs in one bit each get another
   keystream; the same note sealed twice is the same 92 bytes. A sender who publishes one leaf
   twice with two plaintexts reuses a pair on purpose, and loses only what it already knows: the
   recipient never encrypts under these keys, and takes at most the one that passes the leaf check.
5. **What the tag binds.** The leaf is the associated data and an input of the key; `pk_eph` and
   `IVK` are inputs of `s`. Tests on both entry points and on the Rust model: each of the 736 bits
   of an output flipped, bit 255 of `pk_eph` included, and nothing is found; the 28 bytes of one
   note under the leaf of another from the same sender, key and recipient do not open; another
   wallet opens nothing, with its own address or with the recipient's. After the tag, the leaf
   check (`seal.js` l. 128–132, `webcrypto.js` l. 158–162, `notes.rs` l. 318–334): the existing
   test `a note whose plaintext lies about its leaf is ignored` builds the note of 1 unit that
   says 1,000,000. What passes is a leaf whose opening the wallet holds: spendable, for the amount
   and the ticker shown. A note of 0 is kept out of the balance (`keys.js` l. 369).
6. **Scanning.** As §4 says, in the three implementations: low order first, from the bytes; then
   the tag, the ticker, the leaf. The vault names a note by its position (`out:<pos>`, `keys.js`
   l. 366) and never by its leaf.
7. **What stops a scan.** On OpenSSL, on Chrome and in the Rust model, nothing. Test `no output
   makes a scan throw, on either entry point`: every odd encoding as `pk_eph`, 300 random outputs
   and ten malformed ones give "not for this wallet" without an exception; 2,000 random outputs on
   the Rust model. In the vault a scan that throws does not stop the others (`match.js`
   l. 17–43); its leaf is read again at the next sync (`sync.js` l. 117, 150). In Firefox five
   values throw: sealing-2. What a scan costs is sealing-1 and sealing-5.
8. **What an output reveals.** `pk_eph` is a fresh key, `ct` twelve bytes under a key used once,
   the leaf a hash of a key and a `rho` that only the sender and the recipient can compute. Two
   outputs to one address share nothing. The sender knows the note and its tweaks, and not
   `nsk`: it cannot compute `Poseidon(nsk + τn, leaf, pos)`, so it does not see the note spent.
   The holder of `ivk` reads every note of the wallet, past and future, as §1 says, and no
   nullifier. What a leaked per-note key gives is in `SPEC-NOTES.md` §6, as written. This is what
   the bytes on the chain reveal. What the time of a scan reveals is sealing-1.
9. **Public notes.** `selfSecret` and `publicOpening` (`seal.js` l. 143–160) are §5. The vault's
   counter is saved before the key is used (`reserveNote`, `keys.js` l. 311–317), and a restored
   vault reserves none until it has read every leaf and counted the keys its indexers name
   (`#requireChecked`, l. 302).
10. **Unused inputs and outputs.** An unused input is a note of 0 with two scalars from 40 bytes
    of `crypto.getRandomValues` each, reduced into `[1, l)`, and a `rho` drawn the same way
    (`vault/src/spend.js` l. 20–23, 40–45); an unused output is sealed to the wallet's own
    address with its own 32 fresh bytes, like any other (l. 88–104). The three outputs are
    shuffled with the platform's generator (`send.js` l. 23–30).
11. **What an indexer learns.** The sync reads `/status`, every leaf from a position and every
    nullifier from a height, and rebuilds the tree and the paths on the device (`sync.js`
    l. 89–153; `api.js` l. 203–233). Nothing in `vault/src` calls `/path` or `/nullifier/:hex`.
    The positions and heights it asks from say where the wallet stopped reading, not what is its
    own. `/token/:ticker` is asked before a BURN, a LIST and a DEPLOY's burn only
    (`ui/send-panel.js` l. 94–99), never before a TRANSFER. That is what the requests say. What
    their timing says is sealing-1, and what the anchor of a spend says, sealing-9.
12. **The signature.** The nonce is 512 bits of SHA-256 over the tag, the key and the message,
    reduced modulo `l` and refused when 0 (`eddsa.js` l. 21–26, 51–53). One key and one message
    give one signature; another message or another key, another nonce (tested on both entry points). The
    challenge hashes the public key with `R8` and the message, so a signature under a note's
    tweaked key says nothing under another tweak. Signatures stay inside proofs.
13. **Wire v0.4, on paper.** With one `e` for the outputs `j` of a spend,
    `s_j = HKDF(z_j, info = pk_eph ‖ IVK_j ‖ j)`.
    - *No pair repeats.* Two outputs to one address share `z` and differ in `j`, so in `s`, the
      leaf, the key and the nonce. The proposal says so; it holds.
    - *One recipient learns nothing of another's.* A recipient gains `z_j = e·IVK_j` for a key it
      chose, or rather a ciphertext keyed by it. For an honest key `IVK_j = k·9` that value is
      `k·pk_eph`, which anyone with `k` computes from the chain: nothing new. For a key whose
      discrete logarithm it does not know, it cannot compute `z_j` at all, and holds a ciphertext
      it cannot open. For a key of low order `z_j` is zero and the address is refused. Curve25519
      and its twist have no other small subgroup, and `e` is clamped, so no part of `e` is read
      off a small set. And `e` is used for one spend: the attacks that find a static secret from
      many answers `e·P` have at most two points to ask.
    - *What the cited theorem needs.* Bellare, Boldyreva and Staddon prove reuse safe for
      recipients whose keys are known to be well formed. Here an address proves nothing. What
      stands in for it is that each `s_j` is a hash of `z_j` with `IVK_j` and `j`: a key made
      from a victim's `IVK` gives its maker an `s` it cannot compute, and gives the victim an
      output that fails the leaf check under its own `A` and `N`. This is an argument, not a
      proof: §5.
